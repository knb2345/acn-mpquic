Frame-aware multipath QUIC for live lectures

Progress report, prepared by Kabir Bajaj for the team and course staff

We are building a frame-aware scheduler for multipath QUIC, for students who watch live lectures on a phone with two SIM cards. Its core is a learned bandwidth predictor: a small neural network, compiled into TQUIC, that gives a cautious estimate of each path's capacity for the next half second, which the scheduler uses to plan every video frame. Everything runs in emulation on public cellular traces and a real recorded lecture. The final comparison (8 schedulers, 5 scenarios, 5 runs each on the real lecture) is complete. On replayed cellular traces both deadline-aware schedulers miss fewer frames than every transport-only scheduler (10.6% hand-tuned and 13.5% learned, against 16–18%). The learned predictor is the most reliable capacity estimate we measured (it overestimates 11% of the time on unseen traces, against 38% for BBR), but in this first version that has not yet turned into fewer misses than the hand-tuned estimate.

The learned bandwidth predictor

The scheduler's key input is B_p, how fast path p will deliver over the next moments. Simple estimates get this wrong in opposite ways: BBR's estimate and our hand-tuned rule (best rate of the last 3 s) are often too high, while the recent delivery rate is far too low on a path that carries little traffic. An overestimate is what makes a frame miss its deadline.

Model

ml_features.rs, ml_model.rs

An MLP (44 inputs, two hidden layers of 32, 4 outputs). Every 100 ms, per path, it reads the last second of what the sender sees: delivery rate, send rate, queueing delay, loss, plus the current congestion window, bytes in flight, min RTT and BBR's estimate. It predicts the 10th, 20th, 30th and 50th percentile of the path's mean capacity over the next 0.5 s. The scheduler uses the 10th percentile: "at least this much, 9 times out of 10". The weights are compiled into TQUIC as constants.

Training

analysis/ml/, testbed/collect.sh

60 emulated streaming sessions under 20 random network conditions drawn from six training traces (Verizon LTE ×2, T-Mobile LTE ×2, AT&T LTE 2016, Verizon EVDO), plus constant rates and random outages. None of the test traces is used. The labels are exact, because the replay log records the true capacity. Trained with the quantile (pinball) loss in numpy on 51,210 samples from 16 conditions and validated on 13,355 samples from 4 held-out conditions. The quantile the scheduler uses was chosen on 4 further training conditions.

The problem we are solving

Setting

Many students in India attend online classes on a phone over mobile data. Many phones hold two SIM cards from different carriers, so the phone has two cellular links at once. Each link alone is often slow and unsteady: a few Mbit/s, delays of 50–150 ms, and short drops in coverage. Used together, the two links could keep a live lecture smooth even when one of them falters.

Multipath QUIC makes that possible at the transport layer: one QUIC connection runs over several network paths at the same time (the IETF draft is in final review). The connection has a scheduler that decides, for every packet, which path it goes on.

How a live lecture travels

The video encoder turns the lecture into frames, 24 per second in our recording. Every 2 seconds it sends an I-frame, a complete picture (about 55 KB in our lecture). The 47 frames in between are P-frames that only describe what changed (about 8 KB). A frame is only usable once every one of its bytes has arrived.

The player shows frames on a fixed schedule: frame i is due a fixed playback delay after the sender produced it. We use 300 ms. A frame that arrives later has missed its deadline; the picture freezes or glitches, and a late I-frame also damages the P-frames that depend on it.

What transport-only schedulers get wrong

The standard multipath schedulers see bytes, not frames. MinRTT sends each packet on the lowest-delay path that has room; round-robin alternates paths; redundant sends everything on every path; ECF waits for the fast path when that finishes the queued data sooner. None of them knows where a frame starts or ends, or when it is due. That causes two problems:

  • Frames get split across paths. When a frame's packets go over a fast and a slow path, the frame is complete only when its last packet arrives over the slow path, so the slow path sets the frame's delay.
  • Big frames block small ones. A large I-frame keeps one path busy for several round trips, and the P-frames queued behind it wait, even when the other path could deliver them on time.

With two fast paths this hardly matters. With two slow cellular paths there is little room to spare, and every I-frame is a deadline risk.

Worked example: low_bw scenario

A 45 KB I-frame on the 1.5 Mbit/s path takes 240 ms just to transmit, plus 30 ms of one-way delay: 270 ms of the 300 ms budget, with no room for the queue of P-frames already waiting on that path. In our measurements MinRTT and ECF missed the deadline on 47–57% of I-frames in this scenario, while missing under 1% of P-frames.

Questions we answer

  1. How often do transport-only multipath schedulers make lecture frames miss their playback deadline when the two paths are low-bandwidth cellular links?
  2. Can a scheduler that knows each frame's size and deadline do better, and which part of its design makes the difference?

The closest recent work, MoMQ (2026), is also frame-aware and also uses TQUIC, but studies Starlink combined with Wi-Fi. Two similar low-bandwidth cellular paths, the common case for students in India, have not been studied.

Our scheduler

Every frame is sent on its own QUIC stream, and the sender tells TQUIC the frame's deadline. When a new frame is ready, the scheduler:

  1. Estimates the frame's finish time on each path: T_p = (Q_p + S) / B_p + sRTT_p / 2, where S is the frame size, Q_p the bytes already waiting for path p, B_p the path's measured bandwidth, and sRTT_p its smoothed round-trip time.
  2. If a path can finish the frame within 85% of the time left before its deadline, sends the whole frame on the path that finishes first.
  3. Otherwise (usually a large I-frame) splits the frame over both paths so the two parts finish at the same time.
  4. Keeps each frame on the path(s) it was given, so P-frames on the other path overtake an I-frame that is still being sent, instead of queueing behind it.

For B_p we use BBR's bottleneck-bandwidth estimate (the pacing rate overestimates it by up to 25%) and remember each path's best value for 3 seconds, because a path that carries little traffic never shows its full rate. The ablation, frame-pin, keeps steps 1 and 4 but drops the deadline check and never splits, which isolates the value of being deadline-aware.

What we have built

Everything runs on one laptop (WSL2, Ubuntu 24.04) from scripts in our repository, with fixed random seeds and five runs per scheduler and scenario.

Multipath QUIC stack

third_party/tquic, branch team7

Tencent's TQUIC (Rust) v1.6.0, which implements multipath QUIC. Our changes are 9 commits, each small and documented:

  • four new schedulers in TQUIC's scheduler folder: ECF, hand-tuned deadline, frame-pin and learned, plus the predictor's path sampler and the generated model;
  • hooks the frame scheduler needs: an API to pass a stream's deadline, and a check, while a packet is filled, that keeps a frame's bytes on its path(s);
  • a per-path log of every stream frame sent, so we can count each video frame's bytes per path;
  • BBR's bandwidth estimate exposed to schedulers;
  • a fix for a TQUIC bug that stalled whole connections, and a release of frames past their deadline (see Findings).

All 551 original tests and our 15 new ones pass.

Emulated network

testbed/

Two Linux network namespaces, student and server, joined by two virtual cables, path A and path B. On each end of each cable, netem adds half the round-trip delay and tbf limits the rate with a bottleneck queue of 100 ms. Routing by source address pins each QUIC path to its own cable. One command builds it, and it is safe to rerun after a restart.

Checked: ping measures 40.5 ms on a 40 ms path; iperf3 gets 3.56 Mbit/s of TCP data from a 4 Mbit/s path; with different delays per path, each address sees only its own path's delay.

Cellular traces and replay

traces/, testbed/replay.py

Two public traces recorded while driving (Mahimahi project): AT&T LTE as carrier 1 (5.2 Mbit/s on average, sometimes near 1) and T-Mobile 3G as carrier 2 (2.0 Mbit/s on average, no signal 12% of the time). The replay script changes each path's rate every 100 ms. Each run starts the traces at a different, seeded point, the same for every scheduler. The outage scenario cuts path B to zero for 2 s every 30 s.

Checked: iperf3 throughput follows the trace within about 1 Mbit/s each second, and drops to 0 during an outage.

Lecture workload

workload/

A real lecture: a 60 s clip of Harvard CopyrightX Lecture 3.2 (CC BY 3.0), encoded like a live stream (H.264, no B-frames, an I-frame every 2 s, constant quality capped at 3 Mbit/s): 1,441 frames at 1.57 Mbit/s. A synthetic lecture (slides, pointer, webcam inset; 1,800 frames at 1.56 Mbit/s) was used for development and the first comparison. We avoided strict constant-bitrate encoding because it makes every frame about the same size and hides the I-frame problem.

Streamer

streamer/ (Rust)

A sender (the QUIC server) and a receiver (the QUIC client, one path per SIM address). The sender writes frame i to a new stream at its scheduled time and passes its deadline to TQUIC. The receiver records when each frame's stream completes. All timestamps use one monotonic clock shared by both sides.

Per frame we log: size and type, send time, completion time, deadline, and bytes sent on each path, matching the log format in our proposal.

Experiments

testbed/run_stream.sh

One script per scenario runs every scheduler 5 times and rebuilds the network before each run so queues start empty. The final comparison is 8 schedulers × 5 scenarios × 5 runs = 200 runs of 60 s, about 3.7 hours. It runs from a frozen copy of the programs, and each run records the exact TQUIC version it used.

Metrics and analysis

analysis/

Per run: frame completion time (median, P95, P99); deadline-miss rate, overall and for I- and P-frames, at playback delays of 150, 300, 500 and 1000 ms; stall count and total stall time from a player model that waits for late frames; end-to-end delay; peak reordering buffer; and share of bytes per path. Because runs with the same number see the same network, we also compare schedulers run by run. Scripts produce the tables and report figures.

What we found

  1. Transport-only schedulers cost deadlines on cellular links. On replayed cellular traces MinRTT, ECF and round-robin miss 16–18% of frames at a 300 ms playback delay; deadline-aware frame scheduling (hand-tuned or learned) cuts that to 10.6–13.5%. Pinning whole frames without the deadline check (frame-pin) does not help (16.9%), so the deadline check and splitting are what matter.
  2. The learned predictor is the best capacity estimate we measured where conditions change. On unseen traces its cautious estimate overestimates 11% of the time, against 38% for BBR and 47% for the hand-tuned rule, and its median is the most accurate.
  3. Better estimates did not yet mean fewer misses than the hand-tuned scheduler. The learned scheduler is cautious everywhere, steady links included, and moves more traffic onto the weaker path; the hand-tuned rule wins narrowly. Less caution on steady paths, and retraining with the learned scheduler's own traffic, are the next steps.
  4. On fast links the scheduler hardly matters, and on a link too slow for the lecture's I-frames no scheduler fixes it. The playback delay does: 500 ms instead of 300 ms removes almost all misses on low_bw.

Findings along the way

A stall bug in TQUIC's multipath path selection

With path B nearly dead while it was still being validated, whole connections stalled: 15 of 1,800 frames in 90 s, while single-path on the same trace delivered everything. TQUIC kept choosing the path that needed validation frames even though its congestion window was full. Those frames are written after the congestion check, so no packet left on any path, including acknowledgements for path A. We fixed it in a few lines and plan to report it upstream.

Pinned frames and a rare BBR collapse

In one run BBR on path A fell to about one packet per second and never recovered; frames already assigned to that path never arrived (61 of 1,800). The scheduler now lets any frame that is past its deadline use either path, and the rerun scenario had no misses or stalls.

A real lecture at full quality overloads low_bw

Encoded at the same quality as our first tests, the real lecture needs 2.13 Mbit/s, about 90% of what both low_bw paths carry; queues grow without bound and every scheduler missed about 65% of frames. A live platform lowers the bitrate on such a connection, so the evaluation uses 1.57 Mbit/s.

WSL2 steps the system clock

The wall clock jumped forward by about 0.5 s every 30–40 s, which showed up as frames up to 1.1 s late. All timing now uses the monotonic clock. Week 1 transfer times taken before the fix can be about 0.5 s long; byte shares are unaffected.

Transport-only schedulers split frames even when one path is enough

In a 10 MB transfer on the 30 ms / 150 ms scenario, MinRTT still sent 48% of the bytes over the 150 ms path, because both congestion windows stay full. In the streaming runs round-robin split 89–97% of frames across both paths. This is the behaviour the frame-aware scheduler is meant to avoid.

Limitations of the current results

  • All results are from emulation on one laptop (network namespaces on WSL2) with public US cellular traces; there is no live cellular run.
  • The learned predictor is trained on six public traces; how well it transfers to Indian networks is untested.
  • Our TQUIC changes include two bug fixes that apply to every scheduler; each comparison runs all schedulers from one frozen build.

Next steps

  • Make the learned scheduler less cautious on steady paths (e.g. pick the quantile from the predicted spread), and retrain with traffic from the learned scheduler itself.
  • Add figures (miss rate against playback delay, completion-time distributions).
